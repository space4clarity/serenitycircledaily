Leaving a Little Room in the Story About Someone Else

Ordinary friction can invite a quick verdict; compassion leaves room for what we do not know about another person.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
I can be a good human being, although an imperfect one;

Alcoholics Anonymous
Came to Believe

A delayed reply, a sharp tone, or someone taking too long at the counter can become a whole story remarkably quickly. The inconvenience is real, but the explanation we attach to it may be less certain. In daily life during recovery, there can be value in leaving that explanation unfinished. Compassion sometimes begins with admitting that we have only a small part of another person's day.

In the supplied passage from Came to Believe, published by Alcoholics Anonymous, the contributor describes learning to accept personal imperfection while taking responsibility for how to live. Acceptance sits alongside action, including the wish to bring both good judgment and care into relationships. This account is explicitly grounded in A.A. and religious faith. One idea readers with different beliefs can consider is that recognizing human limitations need not end our concern for how people treat one another.

That perspective can extend to the judgments we make about others. If our own imperfect moments need context, someone else's might need context too. This does not require deciding that every hurtful action has an innocent explanation. It asks for a more modest distinction: we can know that something bothered us without knowing everything it reveals about the person who did it. We can address conduct while leaving character less firmly settled.

Sue Johnson's passage from Hold Me Tight approaches compassion through connection. She describes care learned in close relationships extending into concern for people beyond those relationships. Her emphasis on mutual dependence challenges the assumption that other people's struggles belong to a separate world. This is a different emphasis from the A.A. contributor's spiritual account of acceptance and sober living. Read together, the passages invite consideration of how acknowledging our own humanity might widen the humanity we allow others.

Consider a hypothetical afternoon when you have chosen an alcohol-free drink and the person serving you responds impatiently to a question. You might feel exposed, irritated, or tired of making an ordinary request feel complicated. The interaction deserves to be understood from your side. There is also room to leave the server's motive unknown. You can repeat the request, choose another place, or simply finish the transaction without resolving what kind of person they are.

The point is not to replace an angry explanation with a cheerful one. Inventing a reassuring story about someone's difficult day is still inventing a story. Sometimes the most honest position is simply that their tone was unpleasant and their reasons are unclear. That small uncertainty leaves space for frustration without requiring it to carry a larger conclusion. It also leaves practical choices available, including a complaint when one is warranted.

Self-compassion belongs here because quick judgments are themselves ordinary human moments. Catching yourself making one does not need to become another accusation. You may notice that being misunderstood has made you especially alert to dismissive behavior, or that you have little patience available today. Neither observation requires a verdict about your recovery. Whether you are sober, reducing harm, or considering change, you can take your own experience seriously while remaining curious about the limits of what you know.

Neither passage offers proof that this stance changes addiction outcomes. They offer perspectives on living with other people: accepting imperfection while acting with care, and recognizing connections beyond our immediate concerns. An everyday expression of those ideas can be quite small. We can describe what happened accurately, decide what response fits, and allow the person involved to remain larger than the brief encounter we had with them.

A SMALL INVITATION

If you like, notice one explanation you attach to an everyday annoyance and consider which parts you actually know.

SOMETHING TO CARRY WITH YOU

In what ordinary interaction might leaving someone's motives uncertain give you more room to choose your response?
