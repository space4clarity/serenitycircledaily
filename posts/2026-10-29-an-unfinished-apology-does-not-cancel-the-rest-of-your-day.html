An Unfinished Apology Does Not Cancel the Rest of Your Day

A reflection on making room for ordinary enjoyment while taking an unresolved mistake seriously.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
Since I thought trying was not enough, I stopped trying.

Alcoholics Anonymous
Came to Believe

After a mistake, ordinary enjoyment can start to feel inappropriate. A meal tastes good, something makes you laugh, or a friend offers company, and an objection follows: how can you enjoy this when something still needs fixing? In sobriety or while changing a relationship with alcohol or other substances, that question can become another condition placed on daily life. You may feel obliged to stay unhappy until the situation is resolved.

The contributor in Alcoholics Anonymous’ Came to Believe describes believing that forgiveness required an impossible level of goodness. Under that expectation, effort itself lost its value. The account connects that belief with giving up on trying, and later describes finding welcome among people who were compassionate and understanding. Their welcome arrived while the contributor was still struggling with guilt and belief. Acceptance was part of the unfinished story.

That account eventually moves toward faith in God, which matters to the contributor’s understanding of what happened. Readers do not have to share that faith to consider the cost of making perfection a requirement for forgiveness. If nothing short of being entirely good counts, an ordinary attempt at repair can seem too small to matter. Continued unhappiness can begin to look like the only adequate response.

Richard C. Schwartz approaches a related concern from a different perspective in No Bad Parts. He describes vulnerable parts of a person being rejected after they have been hurt, and argues that this rejection also cuts people off from qualities such as playfulness and openness. He questions the expectation that adulthood means losing access to joy. His framework differs from the spiritual account in Came to Believe; both passages nevertheless raise questions about what gets lost when people believe some aspect of themselves makes them unacceptable.

Read together, these perspectives suggest a modest distinction: enjoying something does not settle a responsibility, and an unsettled responsibility does not have to determine every moment. Laughter cannot make an apology sufficient. A pleasant afternoon cannot restore someone else’s trust. But refusing those things does not give the other person back what was lost, either. The relevant work still has its own shape, its own limits, and sometimes another person’s timetable.

Imagine missing a commitment to a friend, acknowledging it, and offering a practical way to make it right. The friend needs time before responding. Later that day, someone invites you to share a meal. Accepting could feel careless, as though you have moved on too quickly. Yet you can remain available for the conversation you owe while eating dinner and enjoying company. You do not need to turn the meal into a discussion of your mistake or recruit anyone to declare you forgiven.

There is also a limit worth respecting: another person may still be angry, and your willingness to enjoy the evening does not require their approval or participation. They get to decide what contact they want. You get to take their response seriously without making every ordinary comfort depend on it. This leaves room for accountability that is specific enough to follow through on, rather than an indefinite sentence of feeling bad.

For someone sober, reducing harm, or finding another path, daily life includes both unfinished business and things worth enjoying. The books offer different ways of questioning whether rejection is the price of taking pain seriously. A useful reflection is whether denying yourself an ordinary good moment serves the responsibility at hand. Sometimes the apology is still pending, the outcome is uncertain, and lunch can still taste good.

A SMALL INVITATION

If you like, notice one ordinary thing you enjoy today without asking it to mean that everything is resolved.

SOMETHING TO CARRY WITH YOU

What ordinary enjoyment feels difficult to allow yourself when you are waiting to make something right?
