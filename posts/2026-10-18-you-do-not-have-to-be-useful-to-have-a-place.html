You Do Not Have to Be Useful to Have a Place

Two recovery perspectives invite a closer look at connection that leaves room for care without making usefulness the price of belonging.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
I began to have concern for others.

Alcoholics Anonymous
Came to Believe

Being helpful can feel like a clear way to secure a place among other people. There is something to do, someone to thank you, a reason you were included. In recovery, that can become a quiet question: would there still be room for me if I arrived without anything to offer? Connection becomes harder to trust when every invitation seems to carry an assignment, even one nobody actually gave us.

In Alcoholics Anonymous’ Came to Believe, the contributor describes a change from deep distrust toward concern for other people. Compassion gradually extends to people with drinking problems and to family members. What matters here is the discovery of an ability to care, rather than a record of helpful deeds. The writer understands this as something newly developing, not simply the recovery of an earlier, better version of life.

That distinction leaves room for someone who cannot point to a past when relationships felt easy. There need not be an old, sociable self waiting to return. Interest in another person can be unfamiliar, tentative, and brief. The contributor specifically describes being able to imagine another person’s position only for short moments. Read as a personal account, this makes room for imperfect attention without turning it into a standard everyone must meet.

Richard C. Schwartz approaches caring from a different direction in No Bad Parts. Within his framework of inner parts and Self, apparently selfless behavior can sometimes be organized around winning approval or avoiding punishment. He also questions the pressure to maximize personal potential. His account makes a useful distinction between activities that matter to someone and activities pursued under pressure to establish their worth. Generosity and the wish to be accepted can exist in the same gesture.

These passages do not offer identical explanations. The Came to Believe contributor understands growing compassion through sobriety, A.A., and spiritual discovery. Schwartz uses his own therapeutic and spiritual framework to describe changing motives and priorities. Neither passage establishes that helping earns belonging. Together, they invite a narrower reflection: care can become part of connection without serving as the admission fee. These are perspectives for considering relationships, not evidence that either approach will produce a particular recovery outcome.

Imagine someone attending a casual gathering who usually handles all the cleanup. This time, they are tired and would like to sit with everyone for a few more minutes. The dishes still matter, and sharing work still matters. But there is also a question about whether this person experiences being present as enough. An ordinary evening can reveal how easily participation becomes a job someone believes they must perform to remain welcome.

Mutual care includes receiving, though receiving may offer less control over how others see us. A person can appreciate company without entertaining everyone, accept a kind message without immediately providing reassurance, or have little to contribute to a conversation. None of that cancels responsibility for how we treat people. It simply leaves space between being accountable in a relationship and continually demonstrating that we deserve one.

Whether someone is sober, reducing harm, beginning recovery, or choosing another path, belonging need not depend on becoming the most generous person in the room. There can be room to contribute and room to have limits. The connection worth considering here allows care to move in more than one direction, with no requirement to arrive already confident, especially useful, or finished figuring things out.

A SMALL INVITATION

If you like, write one sentence about what you enjoy bringing to a relationship besides practical help.

SOMETHING TO CARRY WITH YOU

What would help you feel welcome in a relationship on a day when you have little to offer?
