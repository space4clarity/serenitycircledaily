Taking Responsibility Without Making Yourself the Problem

Responsibility can mean naming what happened and taking on your share, without turning a mistake into a verdict on your worth.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
Stress and responsibility are shifted away from the sick member to others.

Al J. Mooney
The Recovery Book

When a missed commitment affects someone else, taking responsibility can feel tangled up with deciding what kind of person you are. A forgotten errand becomes evidence of selfishness; an unpaid bill becomes a judgment on your entire character. In recovery, that leap can make an ordinary conversation about what needs doing carry the weight of your whole history. Yet the responsibility itself may be specific, even when the feelings surrounding it are large.

In The Recovery Book, Al J. Mooney describes how responsibilities can migrate through a household affected by addiction. Other people pick up unfinished work, cover absences, and adjust their lives around someone else's behavior. His passage also observes that these arrangements do not disappear immediately when someone gets sober. That matters because accountability involves more than acknowledging an incident. It includes recognizing who has been doing the extra work and how long that arrangement has lasted.

Richard C. Schwartz approaches responsibility from another direction in No Bad Parts. He questions a cultural emphasis on willpower that treats unwanted impulses as evidence of an unacceptable inner nature. In his account, self-discipline can become an internal authority that relies on humiliation. He still makes room for firm limits, but describes those limits as compatible with patience and care. His distinction leaves room to take a choice seriously without making contempt the price of admission.

These books do not offer identical accounts of addiction or recovery. Mooney's passage uses a disease framework and directs attention toward family accountability; Schwartz challenges punitive beliefs about human nature and discusses several perspectives on recovery. Read alongside each other, they suggest a useful distinction: other people should not have to carry your responsibilities indefinitely, and you do not have to condemn yourself to acknowledge that they have carried them.

Consider a hypothetical household where someone has repeatedly left grocery shopping to their partner after agreeing to handle it. Calling themselves hopeless does not put food in the kitchen or recognize the partner's lost time. A more concrete acknowledgment would name the missed agreement, recognize the inconvenience, and discuss what they can realistically take responsibility for now. The partner may still be frustrated. A responsible response can leave room for that frustration without asking for immediate reassurance.

This is where self-condemnation can quietly create another demand on a relationship. An apology that becomes a long account of how terrible you are may leave the other person feeling expected to comfort you. That does not mean distress is dishonest or that it must be hidden. It means there can be separate space for your distress and for the consequences someone else experienced. Both deserve attention; neither has to cancel the other.

Gentleness also leaves room for accuracy about capacity. Agreeing to everything out of guilt can create commitments you cannot reasonably keep. Whether you are sober, reducing harm, or beginning to reconsider your relationship with substances, your circumstances matter when making an agreement. A smaller, clear responsibility may be more honest than an impressive promise. Someone else can also decide what they are willing to rely on, without that decision settling your worth.

Forgiveness, when it comes, belongs to the person offering it. Accountability cannot require another person to feel better on a schedule, and self-compassion does not grant permission to dismiss their experience. What remains available is a narrower, more practical task: acknowledge your share, listen to the effect, and be truthful about what you can do next. You can participate in that work while still regarding yourself as someone deserving of care.

A SMALL INVITATION

If you like, write one sentence naming a responsibility you want to acknowledge, keeping the sentence about the agreement rather than your character.

SOMETHING TO CARRY WITH YOU

What would acknowledging your share sound like if you did not also have to judge your whole self?
