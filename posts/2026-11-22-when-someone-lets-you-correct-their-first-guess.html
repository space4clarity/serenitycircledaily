When Someone Lets You Correct Their First Guess

A small correction can tell you something about whether there’s room to be yourself with someone who cares about your recovery.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
or at least that makes you think of something that fits better

Thomas Bien and Beverly Bien
Mindful Recovery: A Spiritual Path to Healing from Addiction

Someone who cares about your recovery can still guess wrong about what matters to you. They may hear frustration where you feel left out, or assume you want company when you want a little time alone. The guess itself may tell you less about the relationship than what happens after you correct it. There’s a particular ease in being able to say that somebody has you wrong and find that they’re still interested in hearing what you mean.

In Mindful Recovery, Thomas and Beverly Bien write about asking a trusted person for their impressions of a dream. They give the dreamer a place in deciding what fits. An impression from someone else can be useful even when it misses, because it can bring a different interpretation to mind. That’s an interesting possibility for ordinary conversation. You may hear someone’s guess about how you feel and recognize, right then, what you were having trouble putting into words.

Sue Johnson is writing about a different kind of exchange in Hold Me Tight. She’s concerned with how partners let each other see what they need and how they respond when the other person takes that risk. She gives weight to the safety a couple has already begun to build. A person who’s unsure how their partner will react may have good reason to hesitate before saying something tender. The response they’ve come to expect is part of what makes speaking feel possible.

The Biens are interested in the meaning a person finds in a dream. Johnson is interested in what happens between partners when one person reaches for the other. Read together, the passages bring a small part of support into view. Somebody offers their understanding of you, and you get a say in whether it fits. If they take your correction seriously, you learn something about what you can tell them the next time they ask how you’re doing.

Imagine you’ve been skipping a regular Friday dinner since you started drinking less. A friend assumes you miss the drinks and offers to pick a place with better alcohol-free options. Hearing that, you realize what you miss most is being included in the loose plans people make as they’re getting up to leave. You tell your friend, and they ask which plans you’ve been missing. The dinner plans can stay undecided for now. You’ve had a chance to describe the part of your social life you miss, and your friend has heard you.

Correcting a kind person can feel awkward. They may have put real thought into their offer, and you may feel tempted to agree with their account because you appreciate the effort. Appreciation and accuracy can sit together in the same conversation. A friend can mean well, and you can give them a clearer picture of your day. It helps when there’s enough ease between you that a correction can be as ordinary as any other detail.

Some relationships have room for that kind of honesty on certain subjects and very little room on others. You get to take that difference into account when deciding what to share. Wherever you are with drinking or using, it can matter to spend time with people who can revise their understanding of you. There’s room in those relationships to be a person whose needs become clearer in conversation, and whose place there can hold through a small misunderstanding.

A SMALL INVITATION

If you feel like it, jot down a small detail someone understood better after you clarified it.

SOMETHING TO CARRY WITH YOU

What helps you feel at ease when you correct someone who cares about you?
