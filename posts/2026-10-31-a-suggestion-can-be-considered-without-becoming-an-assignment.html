A Suggestion Can Be Considered Without Becoming an Assignment

Recovery advice can be something you consider carefully, with room to ask what fits your needs and daily life.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
It is your own direct experience that counts.

Thomas Bien and Beverly Bien
Mindful Recovery: A Spiritual Path to Healing from Addiction

Advice can arrive with more weight than the person offering it realizes. A suggestion about an evening routine, a recovery meeting, or how to spend a weekend can sound like an assessment of your whole life. You may feel pushed to agree before you have considered it, or ready to dismiss it before the person finishes speaking. Between those responses, there may be room to consider the suggestion on its own terms.

In Mindful Recovery, Thomas Bien and Beverly Bien encourage readers to pay attention to what actually works in their lives instead of remaining attached to what is supposed to work. They also caution against turning awareness into another demanding project of self-improvement. Applied to receiving advice, that perspective leaves room for curiosity without requiring an immediate commitment. You can hear an idea and still need time to understand whether it belongs in your day.

Adult Children of Alcoholics / Dysfunctional Families, from the Adult Children of Alcoholics World Service Organization, approaches openness through its Twelve Step framework. The passage describes how suggestions for help can be received as criticism or personal attacks, and it emphasizes becoming willing to learn. That raises a useful question about the moment advice arrives: are you considering what was offered, or already defending yourself against what you believe the offer says about you?

These perspectives have different emphases. The Biens place particular weight on direct experience and gentle awareness. The ACA passage makes stronger claims about its program and connects choice with its understanding of God. Readers do not have to adopt those claims or beliefs to consider one shared concern: an automatic response can leave a suggestion unexamined. Openness can mean giving an idea a fair hearing while keeping your judgment, preferences, and right to decline.

Imagine that someone suggests attending a different recovery gathering because your current one leaves you feeling disconnected. You might hear an accusation that you have chosen badly. You might also agree immediately because disappointing a helpful person feels uncomfortable. A different response could be to ask what they like about the gathering, then consider the timing, atmosphere, and whether you want to go. The conversation can provide information without settling the decision for you.

This matters because advice often mixes a useful observation with a recommendation that may not fit. Someone might notice that your evenings feel lonely, yet suggest an activity you dislike or cannot afford. You can take the observation seriously without accepting the proposed solution. You can also decide that the observation misses something. Considering advice allows for a specific response, including partial agreement, instead of making acceptance or rejection stand for the entire relationship.

There is responsibility on the offering side, too. A person giving advice can leave room for questions, hesitation, and refusal. Their concern does not give them complete knowledge of another person's circumstances. For someone who is sober, reducing harm, or exploring recovery, being willing to listen need not become a requirement to follow every recommendation. The manner in which advice is offered is also part of what you are allowed to consider.

Making room for a different response may mean leaving a conversation with something still undecided. You might find an idea useful later, ask for more information, or discover that it does not suit you. Neither book needs to become a rulebook for that decision. Their perspectives can support a modest distinction: listening is an opportunity to learn something, and deciding what to do remains a separate part of the conversation.

A SMALL INVITATION

If you like, write one sentence about a recent suggestion, naming the part you are curious about or the part that does not fit.

SOMETHING TO CARRY WITH YOU

What would help you give a suggestion a fair hearing while leaving yourself room to decide?
