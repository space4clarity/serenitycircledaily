Listening Without Needing to Get Credit for It

A small concern can invite a different kind of attention when we loosen the need to be seen as helpful.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
Make your home a place for family members to bring their concerns.

Al J. Mooney
The Recovery Book

When someone brings us a concern, two things can happen at once: we hear what they are saying, and we start thinking about how we are coming across. Are we being patient enough? Do they notice that we are trying? In recovery, where relationships may carry a history of disappointment, the wish to be recognized can be especially understandable. But that wish can also compete with the attention the conversation needs.

In The Recovery Book, Al J. Mooney describes the importance of responding sensitively when children bring home everyday problems. His point concerns what a parent makes possible through their response: a child needs somewhere to bring a concern without being scolded or ridiculed. The smaller conversation matters on its own, while also contributing to whether a parent feels approachable later. Attention here is a responsibility, not a reward for bringing an acceptable problem.

Richard C. Schwartz approaches attention from another direction in No Bad Parts. He describes protective parts that can appear caring or polite while working hard to secure other people's approval. Within his framework, even behavior that looks generous can carry a private agenda. He also argues that these protectors deserve compassion. Recognizing a wish for approval does not require attacking ourselves for having it or declaring every kind gesture false.

These passages address different subjects. Mooney offers guidance about family relationships; Schwartz describes inner experience through his model of parts and Self. Read together as perspectives, they raise a useful everyday question: how much room does another person's concern have when we also need the exchange to demonstrate that we are doing better? Neither passage needs to become a claim about what treats addiction for that question to matter.

Imagine someone telling a family member that dinner plans keep changing at the last minute. The family member, who has been working on sobriety, immediately lists everything they have done to become more dependable. That response may express real effort and a real wish to have it recognized. Yet the original concern about dinner has almost disappeared. A different response could stay with the inconvenience long enough to understand which arrangement needs attention.

Making that room does not require flawless listening or hiding every feeling. The wish for recognition can remain present without deciding the whole conversation. There may be another time to discuss effort, discouragement, or the need for encouragement. Allowing the current concern its own space means that someone else does not have to provide reassurance before their practical problem can receive attention.

This distinction can matter whether you are sober, reducing harm, beginning recovery, or finding your own path. When change matters deeply, ordinary feedback can start to feel like an assessment of the entire undertaking. A complaint about a missed message can become an argument about whether anyone appreciates your effort. Keeping the conversation specific leaves something concrete to discuss. It also leaves your larger story unfinished, with no need for this exchange to settle it.

Listening this way can be modest and imperfect. You might still feel defensive, want credit, or think of your answer too soon. Schwartz's perspective offers a way to regard that approval-seeking with care; Mooney's passage keeps attention on the person bringing the concern. Together, they suggest room for a response that acknowledges your own feelings while letting another person's words remain the subject for a little longer.

A SMALL INVITATION

If you like, write one sentence about something you would want to understand better the next time someone brings you an everyday concern.

SOMETHING TO CARRY WITH YOU

What helps you stay interested in someone's concern when you also want them to recognize your effort?
