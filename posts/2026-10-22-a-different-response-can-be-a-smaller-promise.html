A Different Response Can Be a Smaller Promise

Two recovery readings invite us to consider how an ordinary, believable commitment can give a moment of awareness somewhere to go.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
Once, I thought that only unusual circumstances made these moments possible.

Alcoholics Anonymous
Came to Believe

A moment of clarity can arrive with an oversized assignment attached. You notice something you want to change, and suddenly the promise becomes enormous: always be patient, never disappoint anyone, completely rebuild your days. For someone pursuing sobriety, reducing harm, or considering a change in their drinking or drug use, that distance between an honest wish and an unlimited commitment can be hard to live with. A different response might begin with a promise small enough to mean.

In Alcoholics Anonymous’ Came to Believe, a contributor describes powerful spiritual experiences, then reflects on the attention and effort involved in responding to them. The account also recognizes that other people arrive at awareness more gradually. That recognition matters: a dramatic turning point is not the only kind of beginning the passage allows. Its religious language belongs to the contributor’s understanding of life; readers can consider the relationship between insight and response without adopting that belief.

The passage places value on what happens after an experience that feels significant. An insight may deserve attention without supplying a complete plan. There is a practical question here for everyday recovery: what commitment would give this understanding a place in an ordinary day? The answer need not match the intensity of the feeling that prompted it. Something deeply important can have a modest next expression.

Adult Children of Alcoholics / Dysfunctional Families approaches the question through accounts of an inner child and the work of developing trust with oneself. In the supplied passage, a contributor connects trust with recognizing adult limitations and keeping commitments realistic. This offers a useful qualification to the impulse to respond wholeheartedly: sincerity does not make every promise possible. A commitment also has to account for time, obligations, resources, and what a person can actually offer.

These perspectives have different centers. Came to Believe emphasizes a response to spiritual awareness; the ACA passage emphasizes an ongoing relationship with oneself, expressed through attention, limits, and follow-through. They do not establish that one approach works for everyone. Read together, however, they suggest that a meaningful response needs a livable form. Wanting change and deciding what to promise are related decisions, and each deserves care.

Imagine someone who leaves a difficult family dinner determined to handle every future gathering differently. By the next morning, that promise feels impossible to define, much less keep. A smaller commitment could concern the next invitation: checking their schedule before agreeing to attend. That choice leaves plenty unresolved. It still gives a specific concern a specific response, without requiring a new personality or an announcement about how every future dinner will go.

Keeping the scale realistic also leaves room for uncertainty. You may not yet know which relationships you want closer, what support feels comfortable, or how your recovery path will develop. A promise can respect those unknowns. It can have a clear limit instead of claiming authority over your whole future. If circumstances change, reconsidering the commitment honestly can be part of taking it seriously.

Neither reading needs to become another standard against which to measure your worth. Their value here is the question they bring into view: what happens when awareness meets the limits of an actual day? There may be no striking answer. There may simply be one commitment you understand well enough to make freely. You can care about a larger change while letting today’s response remain ordinary, particular, and within reach.

If you like, write one sentence naming a small commitment you could realistically keep within your current circumstances.

SOMETHING TO CARRY WITH YOU

Where in your daily life might a smaller, clearer promise feel more honest than a sweeping one?
