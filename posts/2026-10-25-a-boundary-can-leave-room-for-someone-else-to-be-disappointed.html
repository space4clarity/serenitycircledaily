A Boundary Can Leave Room for Someone Else to Be Disappointed

A relationship can matter deeply even when a limit leaves someone disappointed, and neither person has to become the villain.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
see people for who they are in themselves

Thomas Bien and Beverly Bien
Mindful Recovery: A Spiritual Path to Healing from Addiction

A boundary can be clear and still leave an uncomfortable silence. Someone wanted your company, your time, or your agreement, and you could not offer it on those terms. In recovery, reducing harm, or building a sober daily life, that moment can carry extra weight. You may wonder whether caring about the relationship means finding a way to make the other person happy with your decision.

One useful distinction is between respecting a person's feelings and taking responsibility for removing them. A friend can feel disappointed that you are leaving early. You can care about that disappointment while keeping your departure time. Their feeling does not automatically make your limit unkind, and your limit does not automatically make their feeling unreasonable. There is room for two experiences without requiring one of them to disappear.

In Mindful Recovery, Thomas Bien and Beverly Bien describe how easily we judge people according to whether they meet or frustrate our needs. They ask readers to see others as people with lives of their own. Applied to boundaries, that perspective invites a careful question: Can someone dislike my decision without becoming, in my mind, a selfish or unsupportive person? A single disappointed response may deserve attention without becoming a complete description of the relationship.

Sue Johnson approaches connection from a different direction in Hold Me Tight. Her passage emphasizes the importance of feeling able to depend on a partner. She describes connection as allowing people to acknowledge shortcomings while retaining self-respect, and discusses conflict directed toward solving problems and reconnecting. For this reflection, her perspective makes room for an important possibility: closeness can include friction without every disagreement becoming a verdict on the bond.

These passages do not offer identical accounts of relationships. The Biens challenge a narrow view organized around whether others satisfy us; Johnson emphasizes the significance of attachment needs and dependable connection. Read together, they suggest that a relationship deserves both consideration and limits. Seeing another person's needs clearly does not require meeting every one, just as having needs yourself does not make you entitled to every response you hope for.

Imagine an ordinary situation: you plan to leave a family dinner at eight because a shorter visit fits what you can manage today. A relative is disappointed and asks you to stay. You might acknowledge that they wanted more time together and still leave as planned. No elaborate defense is required. The useful detail is that warmth and departure can happen in the same exchange; affection does not have to be demonstrated by extending the evening.

Of course, disappointment and pressure are different things. Someone expressing sadness is not the same as repeatedly ignoring a stated limit or demanding that you justify it until you give in. Respecting another person's perspective leaves you free to notice how they behave. The point is to leave space for ordinary disappointment while still taking repeated disregard seriously. Connection involves how both people make room for the other's choices.

For someone finding their own recovery path, a limit may concern company, plans, privacy, or how much conversation is possible today. It can be modest and specific. You do not need to prove that the other person is wrong before you are allowed to choose it. And if they are disappointed, you may be able to let that feeling belong to the moment without making it the whole meaning of your relationship.
