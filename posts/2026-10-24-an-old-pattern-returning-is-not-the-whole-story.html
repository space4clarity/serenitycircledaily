An Old Pattern Returning Is Not the Whole Story

When a familiar response returns, patience can mean looking at what happened without erasing everything you have learned.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
Rather than changing your thoughts, mindfulness changes your relationship with your thoughts.

Al J. Mooney
The Recovery Book

An old response can return so quickly that it seems to settle a question about your progress. You start managing someone else’s mood, agree before considering what you want, or judge yourself for having a familiar thought. Then comes another judgment: you should be past this by now. In recovery, that expectation can turn one difficult moment into an assessment of your entire life. Patience begins with questioning whether that moment deserves so much authority.

In The Recovery Book, Al J. Mooney describes mindfulness as a change in how people relate to their thoughts, rather than a requirement to change the thoughts themselves. That distinction offers a useful perspective on repetition. If progress requires an unwelcome thought to disappear permanently, its return will always look like failure. If the relationship matters, there is more to consider: how much credibility you give the thought, what you decide afterward, and whether you can leave some of its conclusions unanswered.

Adult Children of Alcoholics / Dysfunctional Families, published by the Adult Children of Alcoholics World Service Organization, approaches repetition through family roles. Its passage describes ways children become responsible, entertaining, quiet, or blamed within troubled households, and how those roles can persist into adulthood. The text offers the possibility of leaving a role with dignity. That word matters here. An established response can be reconsidered without treating the person who learned it as foolish or defective.

These books are addressing different concerns. Mooney discusses attention and acceptance; the ACA text places recurring behavior within its account of family life and its fellowship’s recovery approach. Neither perspective needs to explain every reader’s experience. Together, they make room for one focused possibility: recognizing an old pattern does not have to become another occasion to condemn yourself. These are perspectives to consider, rather than evidence that a particular practice or program will produce a particular recovery outcome.

For a hypothetical example, imagine someone who is reducing their drinking and has decided to stop organizing every family gathering. A relative sends a worried message, and they immediately volunteer to handle the whole weekend. Hours later, they recognize the familiar responsibility they have taken on. The disappointment is understandable. But the facts are narrower than the verdict that nothing has changed: they made a commitment, noticed a pattern, and now have a practical situation to consider.

Patience in that situation does not require declaring the commitment harmless or canceling it immediately. It could mean giving the actual decision some attention before reaching a conclusion about personal worth. There may be an arrangement to clarify, a limit to communicate, or a responsibility they still choose to keep. The relevant question concerns this weekend. It does not need to expand into whether they will always be responsible for everyone, or whether their recovery counts.

This also leaves room for disappointment without making disappointment the measure of progress. Noticing afterward may feel frustrating when you hoped to notice sooner. You can acknowledge that gap without inventing a deadline for becoming a different person. Being sober, reducing harm, or finding your own recovery path does not require describing every recurring difficulty as a hidden success. Some moments are simply difficult, and their meaning can remain limited.

A patient account of change allows both learning and repetition to appear in the same life. It stays specific enough to recognize consequences, choices, and support without turning each occurrence into a final judgment. An old thought may still sound convincing. An old role may still feel familiar. Neither familiarity nor repetition, by itself, tells the whole story of how you are living now.
