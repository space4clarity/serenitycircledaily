Someone Else’s Timeline Cannot Tell You Where You Should Be

Patience can mean giving your own circumstances a fair hearing when someone else seems further along.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
Make patience and self-acceptance your main practice.

Thomas Bien and Beverly Bien
Mindful Recovery: A Spiritual Path to Healing from Addiction

Recovery can acquire a timetable nobody actually agreed to. Someone else sounds comfortable discussing sobriety. A friend has rebuilt a relationship. Another person seems certain about what comes next. Without much notice, their lives become deadlines for yours. The question shifts from what needs attention today to why you have not arrived where they appear to be. Patience becomes harder when you think you are already late.

In Mindful Recovery, Thomas Bien and Beverly Bien describe mindfulness as something people return to repeatedly, with patience toward their own forgetfulness. They question the pressure to achieve spiritual perfection through effort and frustration. Their discussion concerns awareness, rather than a schedule for sobriety or repairing relationships. Still, it raises a useful question for everyday recovery: what happens when the demand to be further along takes over the attention you could give to your actual life?

Adult Children of Alcoholics / Dysfunctional Families, by Adult Children of Alcoholics World Service Organization, examines another version of that pressure. Its passage describes people comparing their private struggles with how composed others appear. Someone may have experienced meaningful progress and still face difficulties with work, relationships, or daily life. The comparison leaves out those circumstances. It also leaves out whatever the apparently comfortable person has chosen not to share.

These books bring different commitments to the subject. The Biens emphasize a gentle return to awareness within a Buddhist-informed spiritual perspective. The ACA text places considerable weight on participation in its fellowship and a relationship with a Higher Power. Its judgments about insufficient program involvement may not fit every reader’s experience. Their useful overlap here is narrower: harsh self-evaluation can crowd out an honest understanding of where someone is. Neither perspective needs to become a universal timetable or a requirement for belief.

Imagine leaving a recovery gathering after hearing someone celebrate a new job. You are still trying to get through an uneven workweek, and their news brings up embarrassment alongside happiness for them. A borrowed timeline might turn that moment into a conclusion that you have wasted your time. A more patient reading could acknowledge both facts: their news matters, and your employment situation deserves attention. Their milestone supplies very little information about your next decision.

Giving your circumstances a fair hearing does not require declaring that everything is fine. Perhaps a practical problem has been postponed. Perhaps something you once wanted no longer fits. Perhaps you are disappointed, without yet knowing what you want to do about it. Those possibilities deserve different responses. Ranking yourself against another person compresses them into the same accusation of being behind, which offers little help with understanding any of them.

This matters whether you are sober, reducing harm, beginning recovery, or finding an approach that fits your life. Time spent trying does not create an obligation to look settled. Nor does someone else’s visible success establish what your days should contain. You can respect their effort without using it to dismiss your own circumstances. You can also want something to change without treating the time it has taken as evidence against your worth.

Patience, in this sense, leaves room for a more accurate question. Instead of asking whether your life looks advanced enough, you might consider which concern is asking for attention on its own terms. The answer may be ordinary and unresolved. It may have nothing to do with the achievement you just heard about. Allowing that difference gives your own life a place in the conversation, including the parts that do not fit anyone’s expected schedule.

A SMALL INVITATION

If you like, write one sentence naming a circumstance that a comparison with someone else leaves out.

SOMETHING TO CARRY WITH YOU

What part of your own circumstances would you want understood before anyone judged your pace?
