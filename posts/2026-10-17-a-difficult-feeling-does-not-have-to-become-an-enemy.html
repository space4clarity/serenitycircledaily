A Difficult Feeling Does Not Have to Become an Enemy

Two perspectives on self-compassion offer room to take a difficult feeling seriously without letting it decide your next choice.

3 minute read

A LINE TO BEGIN
be sure to spend a generous amount of time and energy on yourself

Thomas Bien and Beverly Bien
Mindful Recovery: A Spiritual Path to Healing from Addiction

A difficult feeling can arrive with a second difficulty: the belief that you should not be having it. Resentment becomes evidence that you are unkind. Fear becomes a personal failure. A craving becomes something you think you must condemn before anyone can trust your intentions. For someone navigating sobriety, reducing harm, or considering a change, that extra judgment can make an already complicated moment feel like a verdict on their whole life.

In Mindful Recovery, Thomas Bien and Beverly Bien discuss kindness toward yourself in the context of difficult workplace relationships. Their emphasis is on allowing yourself substantial care before expecting yourself to offer kindness to someone who has hurt you. They resist putting compassion on a deadline. Applied to everyday recovery, this perspective leaves room for an uncomfortable truth: you may want to respond generously and still feel angry, depleted, or unwilling to engage.

Richard C. Schwartz approaches inner conflict differently in No Bad Parts. He describes how people can become caught up in protective reactions, including reactions that attack their own fear. Within his framework, a frightened aspect of a person can become easier to regard with care when it no longer occupies their entire perspective. The useful question for this reflection is whether disliking a feeling has become another source of hostility toward yourself.

These books do not offer identical accounts of compassion. The Biens emphasize giving self-directed kindness time and attention within a Buddhist-informed practice. Schwartz describes compassion as already belonging to what he calls the Self, available when competing inner reactions loosen their hold. Those are distinct spiritual and psychological perspectives, rather than evidence that a particular approach treats addiction. Both, however, challenge the expectation that harshness toward yourself is a necessary starting point for change.

Consider a hypothetical evening when someone working toward sobriety receives a dismissive message from a friend. They feel hurt, then notice an urge to drink, then become angry with themselves for having that urge. Several things are happening, but none has to stand for the whole person. The hurt deserves acknowledgment. The urge matters because it bears on a choice they care about. The self-criticism does not have to become the final authority on what this moment means.

Making room for a feeling is different from granting every impulse permission. Anger does not settle whether to send a message. Loneliness does not decide whether a particular invitation is welcome. A craving does not erase an intention to stay sober or make a less harmful choice. Compassion can mean taking the difficulty seriously while keeping the next decision open. There is no requirement to feel peaceful, grateful, or fully accepting before making that decision.

Care for yourself also leaves room for boundaries with other people. The Biens explicitly place self-kindness first when discussing hurtful working conditions. Schwartz emphasizes seeing vulnerability beneath another person's protective behavior. Seeing that possible vulnerability does not establish what someone else intended, and it does not obligate you to remain available to them. Understanding another person and limiting contact can coexist. So can caring about a relationship and declining a conversation today.

The focused possibility here is modest: a hard feeling can receive consideration without becoming either an enemy or an instruction. You do not have to admire it, explain it completely, or make it disappear. Whatever shape your recovery takes, there can be room to acknowledge that something is difficult and still consider what matters next. Self-compassion need not add another standard you must meet before you deserve support.
